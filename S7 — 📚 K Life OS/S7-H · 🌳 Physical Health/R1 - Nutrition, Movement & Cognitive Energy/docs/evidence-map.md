# Evidence Map

## Purpose

This map defines the first evidence questions for `S7-H-R1`.

It should help separate:

- public-health guidance
- observational evidence
- intervention evidence
- personal self-tracking
- supplement claims
- medical decisions

## Physical activity

### Working evidence anchor

Adults generally benefit from regular aerobic activity and muscle-strengthening activity.

Initial guideline anchors:

- WHO physical activity guidelines: 150-300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity or 75-150 minutes of vigorous activity weekly, plus muscle-strengthening activity.
- CDC adult activity overview: at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity weekly and 2 days of muscle-strengthening activity.
- American Heart Association recommendations: similar weekly aerobic targets, strength training at least 2 days per week, and less sedentary time.

### Research questions

- What activity mix is realistic and sustainable?
- How do walking, running, strength training, and home workouts affect energy, mood, and focus?
- What minimum effective routine gives the best adherence?
- How should soreness, fatigue, sleep, and recovery be tracked?

## Nutrition

### Working evidence anchor

Healthy dietary patterns are usually described through food patterns rather than single magic foods.

Initial guideline anchors:

- WHO healthy diet: at least 400 g fruit and vegetables per day for people older than 10, with attention to fats, salt, and sugars.
- NHS balanced diet: fruit and vegetables, starchy carbohydrates, protein sources, dairy or alternatives, and moderation of high fat/sugar/salt foods.
- Harvard Healthy Eating Plate: vegetables and fruits, whole grains, healthy protein, healthy oils, water, and variety.

### Research questions

- What comfortable meal structure supports energy without feeling punitive?
- Which foods improve satiety, mood stability, and cognitive comfort?
- What happens when protein, fiber, hydration, meal timing, or ultra-processed food exposure changes?
- How can subjective response be tracked without turning food into anxiety?

## Nutrition and cognition

### Working evidence anchor

Diet quality and cognitive health are studied through long-term dietary patterns, metabolic health, vascular risk, inflammation, sleep, microbiome hypotheses, and lifestyle context.

Initial literature anchors:

- Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, and MIND-style patterns are often associated with better cognitive aging outcomes in review literature.
- Evidence is not the same as a guaranteed individual cognitive effect.
- Short-term focus and mood can be affected by sleep, energy intake, hydration, caffeine, stress, and expectations.

### Research questions

- Which nutrition patterns correlate with better self-rated focus and mood?
- Which variables are confounders: sleep, stress, cycle phase, caffeine, workload, illness, training load?
- What is a reasonable weekly reflection format?

## Supplement safety

### Working evidence anchor

Supplements should be treated as risk-managed inputs, especially when dosing, deficiency status, interactions, or health conditions matter.

Initial source anchors:

- NIH Office of Dietary Supplements vitamin D fact sheet gives recommended intakes and safety context.
- NIH and National Academies sources identify 4,000 IU/day as a common adult tolerable upper intake level for vitamin D unless a clinician directs otherwise.
- Magnesium, omega-3, and nootropic blends require separate source review, especially for GI effects, bleeding risk, sedation, thyroid/autoimmune issues, medication interactions, pregnancy, and liver/kidney considerations.

### Research questions

- Is there a lab-confirmed deficiency or only a perceived need?
- What is the dose, form, duration, and stop rule?
- What symptoms or adverse effects should trigger stopping and clinician review?
- Which claims are supported by human evidence, and which are marketing?
