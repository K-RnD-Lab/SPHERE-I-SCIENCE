## Why this case exists

This case documents a real nutrition/supplement safety lesson: AI-generated wellness advice can make supplementation feel more precise and harmless than it actually is.

The relevant issue is not one specific product. The issue is the pattern:

- using AI output as if it were a treatment plan
- changing supplement form, amount, or timing without a clinician-aware review
- treating food-based intake and supplement intake as interchangeable
- tracking short-term subjective effects without enough context
- letting tables or seasonal notes create false certainty

## Case summary

A supplement-oriented plan included magnesium, omega-3, vitamin D3, and cognitive-stack ideas. During review, it became clear that:

- the magnesium form and total intake question needed more caution
- food intake could already contribute meaningfully to magnesium and omega-3-related nutrition goals
- omega-3 wording created confusion between "zero capsules" and "zero omega-3 intake"
- D3 is the more season-sensitive topic, while omega-3 is mainly a diet/fish-intake question
- personal symptoms and sleep changes should be treated as stop/review signals, not as proof that a dose is "working"

No dosing protocol from this case should be copied into public guidance.

## Omega-3 wording correction

Use this distinction:

```text
0 capsules != 0 omega-3 intake
```

For this research line, omega-3 should be tracked as:

- food-based omega-3 sources
- fish or seafood frequency
- seed/nut sources where relevant
- capsule use, if any
- reason for capsule use
- tolerance and adverse effects

The phrase "0 in summer" is too ambiguous. A safer phrasing is:

```text
omega-3 capsules: not used when food pattern is sufficient
omega-3 intake: tracked through fish/seafood and plant sources
```

## Seasonal interpretation

Do not over-attach omega-3 to season.

Season matters more clearly for vitamin D exposure because sunlight and UVB availability change through the year.

For omega-3, the better question is:

```text
Is there enough relevant food intake this week, or is there a clinician-supported reason for supplementation?
```

## Magnesium lesson

Magnesium needs form-aware and source-aware tracking.

Track:

- magnesium from food patterns
- supplement form
- elemental magnesium amount if a supplement is used
- digestive tolerance
- sleep, anxiety, sedation, stimulation, and other subjective effects
- medication and kidney-function considerations when relevant

Do not treat "magnesium" as one simple input. Different forms, doses, contexts, and personal health factors can change risk and tolerance.

## AI-advice risk pattern

AI can help organize questions, but it should not be treated as a prescriber.

Red flags:

- a plan includes multiple supplements at once
- the output gives confident dose-like instructions
- the plan mixes deficiency treatment, wellness optimization, and cognitive enhancement
- one symptom is used to justify continued experimentation
- the plan does not ask about labs, medications, health history, pregnancy, surgery, kidney/liver issues, bleeding risk, or clinician review

## Research rules created from this case

1. Food-first baseline before supplements.
2. One variable at a time.
3. No public personal dosing protocols.
4. Track capsule use separately from nutrient intake.
5. Treat symptoms as safety signals, not optimization feedback only.
6. Use source registry before changing supplement form or dose.
7. Escalate D3, magnesium, omega-3, and nootropic-stack decisions to clinician-aware review when dose, deficiency, symptoms, or interactions matter.

## Public boundary

This case can be used as a research-safety lesson.

It should not be used as:

- a supplement routine
- a vitamin D protocol
- a magnesium protocol
- an omega-3 protocol
- a cognitive-stack recommendation
- medical or nutrition advice
